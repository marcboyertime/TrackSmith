This is nothing new: recording itself has been putting musicians out of work since 1920. Every gig lost to a DJ is a musician out of work: every bar with a jukebox instead of a band is a musician out of work: every party where records are played instead of live music is a musician out of work. This is just unmistakably clear: recording has always reduced the number of jobs for musicians. In the 1940s, the NY musicians union instituted a ban on recording, to try and stop this very process. I love playing live, and there's no substitute for it. Or I can use string samples, or horn samples. That empowers one person--me--at the expense of another--the drummer. That's what recording technology has always done.

it's not the fault of "kids today:" it's not that they suck or lack integrity or some such nonsense. It's that technology augments the process of deskilling, and it's beneficial for a lot of people and bad for others. Automobiles were terrible for carriage makers.

Yah whatever rot is going on, been going on awhile. When I joined the musician union about 1969 or 1970 my local had only recently halted a long tradition of requiring an (easy) proficiency exam in order to join. Too many fellas were getting gigs, who couldn't pass the test. So from then on, anybody who wanted to be a union musician only had to claim to be a musician and pay the fees.

Can't recall when they changed the following bylaw, or maybe its still on the books-- Back in the 1960's or earlier the old guys in charge of the union decided that an organist with a left foot was putting a bassist out of work, and anybody with a crude transistor beat box that sounded worse than a pong machine, was putting a drummer out of work.

So even up into the 1980's if there wasn't any better work available with a group, had to pay dues on double scale as punishment for putting a bassist out of work with my left foot and putting a drummer out of work with a cheezmaster deluxe drum box, solo playing tiny joints barely big enough to set up a few keyboards. No place for the drummer or bassist, except sitting in a customer's lap.

After early 1980's I managed to always work with at least a guitar and drums which was more fun. The guitarist would steal the bassist's job half the time playing bass, and my left hand would steal the bassist's job the rest of the time. Maybe some folks would actually prefer doing a solo surrounded by machines, but its lots more fun to have real musicians to work with. Whatever an employer can afford to pay for.

Well, I just kicked bass for a few periods in the late 70's early 80's paying the rent solo. Wasn't very good kicking pedals, but wanted both hands free for chords and melody. Built an E to G controller out of pratt-reed pedal parts and initially drove an arp odyssey with CV-Gate. But the gadget used a digital contact scanner, therefore a few months later it was easy to build a digital mono bass synth circuit to install in the pedals chassis, driven by the four bit word from the pedal scanner. No cpu, just cmos digital chips and some opamps and transconductance amps.

Didn't have much money, spent a few hundred on parts, but not near what moog taurus pedals would have cost. And I liked my pedal tone better. More like a mellow plucked bass than a growly analog synth. And it had the "ideal" short pedal note range, same as the low 16 notes of four string electric bass.

Maybe key bass was annoying, but I extremely enjoyed playing keybass. Did it from the 1960's until today. Wasn't very good at making both hands cooperate on a single task, but was slightly better doing two different things with two hands.

Its kinda the dichotomy between what you enjoy doing versus what you have the aptitude to do. I had better aptitude on other occupations, and rather poor aptitude for music, but music playing was fun and interesting. Managed to work steady, but I was just a hack.

Anyway, the occupational issues you mention are hardly new and I generally agree with your ideas. Back in the day it was kinda tough on highly skilled flint chippers trying to compete against newfangled copper knives.

Jack Williamson's 1947 SF short story "With Folded Hands" seems relevant to de-skilling. Another example which comes to mind is an early 1950's Kurt Vonnegut novel, "Player Piano".

Both extrapolate scenarios where virtually EVERYONE has been de-skilled. And the idea that people need SOMETHING to work at, even though it may have become more efficient for machines to do the work while people do little or nothing useful.

Maybe we are gradually drifting toward a Player Piano world, if it doesn't all fall apart before we get all the way there.

I just enjoy doing stuff myself even if it might be more efficient with better results to hire out the work.

I'm not offended, and I'm not interested in being either an ME or a professional. Also I said multiple times that I don't think it replaces a mastering engineer entirely or is as good.

It's part of a process well known in labor history, as I mentioned, which historians call "deskilling." It's never an isolated phenomenon: machine weaving replaced hand weaving as part of wide range of changes in the weaving business: growing shipping marketing: changes in the nature of the customer base.

That's what's happening here. Whether or not it's as good is not the right question to ask, just like it wasn't the right question to ask about hand weaving vs machine weaving. There's a much larger shift in the entire production process--making, marketing, selling-- that this part of. It will cost mastering engineers some work.

An interesting experiment-- I might try this when I get around to testing landr, but it would be interesting to hear of others' findings.

Prepare a float wav file peaking at -6 dB, then run it thru landr at the low intensity setting, and retrieve a float WAV file from landr.

Then take that processed float WAV file, do nothing except attenuate it back down to -6 dB max peak, and send the renamed float WAV file back thru landr a second time.

If landr knows "exactly what it wants to accomplish" then it might be expected that the second pass thru landr ought to be so similar to the first pass thru landr that the two versions ought to nearly null.

In other words, on the first pass-- If landr operates so as to identify a set of "bad" issues and then it fixes the identified issues according to its operational specifications-- Then on the second pass, landr might be expected to recognize the file "as good as its gonna get" except for overall gain. Therefore landr would only apply +6 dB gain to the second pass, and do nothing else.

Every time one would take the landr output, attenuate by -6 dB, and then crank the output back thru landr, theoretically there ought to be minimal change except very slight build-up of noise and distortion due to minor rounding errors? The tenth processing pass ought to be nearly identical to the first processing pass?

On the other hand, if every subsequent pass thru landr would yield measurably changed results, then that would be interesting as well.

Multi-pass drift might be evidence of "non adaptive" canned behavior such as "always scoop the mids" or whatever. Or alternately it might be evidence of landr trying to get "closer to its ideal" on every pass. If each pass thru happens to be different, then would the result tend to get better or worse with each generation?

By analogy, a midi timing quantization algorithm which doesn't absolutely set all notes to fit the timing grid-- Instead, the quantization might move all notes 50 percent closer to the timing grid. With such an algorithm, multiple applications of timing quantization would get ever closer to the rigid hard-quantized final result.

So if landr is programmed to process a file "partway" toward hard compliance to "ideal specifications for the genre" then multiple passes might drift ever-closer the the genre specifications mean (as understood by landr and its programmers).

It might be reasonable to program landr to only go "part way there" toward a genre mean, in order to preserve some variation in each song. Just somewhat "tighter" toward the genre mean.
